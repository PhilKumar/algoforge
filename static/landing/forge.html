Chapter I · The Port

Sakata, 1750.

Boats came down the river loaded with rice, and the whole of Japan learned what it was worth from the men standing on this quay. The world's first futures market ran here — on paper, weather, and rumour.

Chapter II · The Merchant

One man kept better notes than everyone else.

Munehisa Homma traded rice in Sakata and did something nobody bothered with: he wrote down what the price did every single session, for years, and read the record back.

He wasn't looking for the news. He was looking for the shape of the crowd — where it panicked, where it got greedy, where it exhausted itself.

Chapter III · The Ledger

Four numbers, written by candlelight.

Where it opened. Where it closed. The highest it reached, and the lowest it fell. Nothing else was recorded, because nothing else was needed.

Chapter IV · The Shape

Then he drew it as one mark.

A body between the open and the close, a wick to each extreme. A full session of fear and conviction, compressed into a single brushstroke — and still, 275 years later, nobody has improved on it.

What both desks are running
Geometry

A trendline stands until price closes above it. Fibs are measured from the leg the crowd actually broke. The rule is the same on a rice board and on a perpetual.

The Ladder

One entry is a guess. A ladder is a plan — each rung armed below the last low, sized so the round clears its own cost before it claims a profit.

The Ledger

Homma's real edge was the record. Every fill, fee and exit is written down, joined back to the chart it happened on, and kept after the campaign closes.

The hand that never blinks

Homma read one market.
These desks read two.

Same shapes. Same crowd. One that closes at half past three, one that never does — both acted on in milliseconds, with the discipline he had to hold in his own head.
